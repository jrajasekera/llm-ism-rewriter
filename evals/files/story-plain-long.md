# The Grange Calendar

Ines Varga opened the shop at seven, as her father had, and turned on the lamp over the type cabinet before she turned on anything else. It was the second week of October. The Pellham Ferry Grange calendar was due at the hall by the first of November, twelve months and a cover, four hundred copies. She had set two of the months.

Varga Printing stood on Tull Street between a shuttered shoe repair and the feed store. Her father had bought the building in 1979 with the press already in it, a platen press from the Halloran works, black iron with a flywheel as tall as her chest. He printed the Grange calendar on it that first winter and every winter after. Forty-four calendars hung in a row along the back wall, each on its own nail.

There had been other work once. Auction bills and wedding invitations had carried the shop for thirty years, until a copy center opened in Wexler and the brides began ordering from their telephones. This year the calendar was the only job on the books that needed the press at all.

Her father had died in March. In the hospital, two days before, he had asked her to keep the shop going and to keep the calendar coming out, and she had said she would. He had made her say it twice. She had said it the second time with her hand on his arm, and he had nodded and gone to sleep.

The mail came at ten. There was a letter from Dunmore Savings about the line of credit, and she read it standing at the counter. The bank would not renew in January. The balance was eleven thousand dollars, and the shop had cleared less than that in the whole of the summer. She folded the letter along its creases and put it in the drawer with the other one.

The other one was from a woman named Coral Beame at the Wexler County Historical Society. The society was fitting out a working print room in the old depot at Wexler, thirty miles down the river, and it wanted the Halloran and all the type that went with it. The figure she named would pay off the bank and leave some over. The letter had been in the drawer since August. Ines had not answered it.

She worked through the afternoon on March. Each day of the month was a small block of numerals locked into a grid, and the Grange wanted the dates of its suppers set in beside them. At five the light went. She was afraid. She locked the front door and sat on the stool beside the press until the streetlamp came on across the road.

* * *

In the morning her hands were shaking. She noticed it when she tried to pick a hairline rule out of the case and could not get her fingernail under it. She held both hands out flat over the imposing stone and watched them. She had slept well enough. She had eaten. She drank a cup of tea and went back to the case.

The telephone rang a little before nine. It was a man from Dunmore Savings, wanting to know if she had received the letter and if she would like to come in and discuss her options. She said she had received it. She said she would call him back. When she hung up her hands were no worse than they had been before the call, and no better.

She decided to move the ten-point case to the stone so she could work sitting down. A full case of type weighs about as much as a small child and has to be carried level. She slid it out of the cabinet and got it as far as the end of the counter. Then her fingers opened. She did not decide to let go and she did not feel them go. The case hit the floor on one corner and turned over.

Type went everywhere. It ran under the cabinets and out as far as the front mat, thousands of pieces of it, every letter of the font mixed with every other. Her father had called that a pie. In forty-four years he had made two, and he remembered the dates of both.

She stood and looked at it. The calendar needed that face for the supper notices, and there was no second case of it. One person sorting it back by hand would need most of two weeks. She did not have two weeks to give to it, so she went to the telephone and called Dell Ruckert.

Dell had fed the Halloran for her father for twenty-six years and had retired when his knees quit. He listened to her describe the floor. "Don't sweep," he said. "Whatever you do, don't sweep. I'll be there after lunch."

* * *

He came every day that week. They picked the type up off the floor on their hands and knees, which took Dell a long time, and heaped it in baking pans. Then they sat on either side of the stone and put it back in the case one letter at a time. Dell read the face of each piece with his thumb as much as with his eyes. His knees were bad but his hands were fine. Ines's hands had steadied by the second day, and she did not mention them to him.

The pans were her father's. Dell told her about the second pie, in 1991, when a delivery boy backed a hand truck into the cabinet. Her father had not spoken for an hour. Then he had walked across to the feed store and bought every baking pan on the shelf.

On Thursday a green station wagon parked in front and a woman in a canvas jacket came in. She was about sixty, with short gray hair and ink ground into the creases of her knuckles. She said she was Coral Beame. She said she had business at the feed store and hoped it was all right to stop, since she had never had an answer to her letter.

"I haven't decided anything," Ines said.

"That's all right. Could I look at it?"

Ines let her. Coral Beame walked around the Halloran once with her hands in her jacket pockets. She asked if she might turn it over, and when Ines nodded she took hold of the flywheel and pulled it through by hand, slowly, listening. She found the oil hole on the side arm that everyone missed and looked into it. She said the press had been well kept.

"We'd run it," she said. "I want you to know that. We'd have the school groups in on Fridays and let every child pull a proof. I don't hold with presses behind ropes."

"My father told me to keep it going here," Ines said.

"I understand." Coral Beame wrote a telephone number on the back of a card and left it on the counter. "The offer stands till the end of the year. After that the board spends the money on a roof."

When she had gone Ines stayed by the press. The bed was faintly warm from the lamp that hung above it. She had told her father she would keep the shop and the calendar, and she had kept them for seven months at a loss. She knew what the numbers said. But she believed that as long as she went on doing what she had told him she would do, he was still somewhere in the building, the way warmth stays in iron after the lamp is switched off. If she stopped, the iron would cool. That was how it seemed to her, and she did not examine it further.

Dell had not looked up from the pans. "Lowercase e," he said, and held one out to her.

* * *

Her brother drove up from the city on Sunday. Anton was three years older and sold commercial insurance. As a boy he had been made to sort spacing material on Saturdays, and he had left for college the week he turned eighteen. He had not set foot in the shop since the funeral. He had on a good wool coat and he kept it on. He looked at the pans of type on the stone. Then he read the bank's letter, which she had laid out on the counter because he had asked to see it.

"Sell it," he said. "Ines. Take the woman's money."

"I told him I wouldn't."

"You told him you'd keep it going. I was in the room." Anton put the letter down. "Here is what I think, and I'll say it once. Dad was frightened. He'd never been frightened in his life and he didn't know what to do with it, so he grabbed the nearest thing, which was you, and he made you swear. I don't believe a frightened man's asking ought to bind anybody. He'd have been ashamed of it a week later, if he'd had a week."

She was angry with him. The anger was hot and plain, and all of it was pointed at her brother. She felt furious at him, standing there across the counter. It was close to rage, what she felt, and Anton was the whole object of it.

"You have a long drive," she said.

He went. At the door he said he would cover the bank himself if she wanted, as a loan, though he thought it would be money thrown in the river. She said she would let him know. She watched his car to the end of Tull Street. Then she went back to the stone and sorted type until her eyes gave out.

* * *

The case was whole again by the twenty-fourth. They printed the calendar over the last four days of the month, Ines locking up the forms and Dell on a stool at the feed board, because he could not stand for a run. He fed each sheet to the guides with two fingers and lifted it off again as the platen opened, four hundred times a page, in the rhythm he had kept for twenty-six years. The shop smelled of ink and kerosene. They ran past the Grange's deadline by a day, and nobody at the hall complained.

December was the last form. When the counter reached four hundred Dell threw the lever and the flywheel ran down by itself, slower and slower, until it stopped with the platen open. Neither of them spoke while it turned.

"He'd have liked that one," Dell said. He was looking at the top sheet. "Good even color."

"Dell, I can't do another year. I've gone over it every way there is."

"I know you can't. I've known since I saw the floor." He wiped his fingers on the rag, one at a time. "I'll tell you what it is, though. You keep your word to Tibor and it's like he hasn't all the way left. That's what the promise does for you. So long as you're doing what you said, he's still about the place."

She said she supposed that was right.

She carried the December sheets to the drying rack. Holding to what she had told her father was a way of holding on to him. While the promise was being kept he stayed close by, and she had known that for some time. She slid the last sheets into the rack and turned off the motor at the wall.

Dell got his coat. At the door he stopped and looked back down the length of the shop at the forty-four calendars on their nails. "Forty-five," he said. "You'll want to hang this one before you do anything else."

She hung it that night, on a new nail at the end of the row. In the morning she took the card from the counter and called the number on the back.

* * *

The riggers came from Wexler on the ninth of December with a flatbed truck and a set of steel skates. Coral Beame came with them. She had the bill of sale in a folder with a cashier's check for the figure in her letter. Ines signed at the counter with her father's pen. Anton had offered to drive up for it, and she had told him there was no need.

It took four men most of the morning. They jacked the Halloran a quarter inch at a time and slid the skates under its feet, then walked it across the floor with pry bars while Coral Beame called the inches. The front window had to come out. Ines held the door and kept out of the way. When the press reached the sill one of the men asked if she wanted a minute, and she found that she did not need one.

The type cabinets went after it. Before they took the ten-point she opened the case and looked at it, every compartment full, the e box heaped higher than the rest. Then she let it go. She kept back one thing, her father's composing stick, with the last line she had set still in it. It was a supper notice for the nineteenth of December, and it asked everyone to bring a dish.

Coral Beame shook her hand at the curb. "First Friday in February," she said. "We'll have the fourth grade from the Wexler school. Come down and show them how to feed it. Nobody on my board can do it without losing a sheet."

"I'll come," Ines said.

The truck pulled out slowly with the press chained upright on the bed under a tarpaulin. She watched it to the end of Tull Street, as she had watched Anton's car. Then she went inside. The shop was larger without the press, and brighter with the window out. There was a clean dark rectangle on the floor where the Halloran had stood for longer than she had been alive. She had expected the room to feel emptied of him, and it did not. The forty-five calendars were on their nails and the stick was on the counter. She got the broom and swept the rectangle, which she could do now that there was no type on the floor to lose.

She had kept her promise because keeping it kept her father near her, and for seven months that had been reason enough. Now she understood that she could set the promise down and he would stay. He had been in the shop because she remembered him there, and she would go on remembering him. Letting the press go had cost her less than she had feared. The promise had done its work, and she could let it rest.
