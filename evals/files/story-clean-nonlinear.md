# The Long Way Round

My aunt Ilse kept forty-one years of bus passes in a shortbread tin, in date order, with a rubber band around each decade. I found them on the first day, in October 2016, under the sink, behind the bleach. I was thirty-nine and holding a roll of bin bags, the heavy-duty kind, because I'd assumed the job would be heavy. It was mostly paper.

You should know about the flat that it had one armchair and the armchair faced the window, which faced the bus stop. She'd timed the walk down at ninety seconds, stairs included. In my lifetime she never drove anywhere. If you asked, she said the bus gave her reading time, and the evidence backed her up: there was a copy of Middlemarch by George Eliot on the arm of the chair with a bus ticket in it about two thirds of the way through, and the spine had been mended with the grey tape plumbers use. It was the same copy she'd had when I was a boy. She once told me she was in no hurry to find out how it ended, which was a joke, I think.

My mother rang at eleven to tell me what to keep. My mother is Ilse's younger sister by four years and lives by the sea now, since her knees went. "The clock," she said. "The good scissors. Anything with Mutti's handwriting on it." I asked about the bus passes. There was a pause long enough that I checked the phone was still lit. "Oh, keep those," she said. "She'd haunt you."

I kept them. They're in my hall cupboard now, next to the fuse box.

* * *

In the spring of 1994 I was seventeen and my mother announced that Ilse would be teaching me to drive. "She's the best driver in the family," my mother said, which I took at face value, the family being three people and one of them me. The car was my mother's Volvo 240, brown, with a cassette player that ate every tape except one. Ilse came over on the 14 bus on Sunday mornings, and at the end of each lesson I parked on our drive and she walked back to the stop.

The one tape was Glen Campbell. Ilse kept it cued to Wichita Lineman and she'd rewind it at junctions, holding the button down and counting under her breath, because the counter was broken and she'd worked out that the start of the song was eleven seconds of rewind from the end of it. I've heard that song perhaps four hundred times and never once by choice. She didn't sing along. She listened to it like somebody checking a sum.

She taught from the passenger seat with her handbag on her knees and her right hand resting on my left wrist, which is a terrible place to rest a hand on someone who's learning to change gear, and we managed. The system was one tap for slow down and two taps for go. She didn't like talking while the car was moving (talking was for lay-bys) so whole lessons went by in taps and Glen Campbell. At the dairy junction, where the lorries come round blind from the right, I'd sit with the indicator ticking until I felt two taps, and then I went, and there was always room.

She could hear the wrong gear before I could feel it. She knew the stopping distance of that Volvo in the wet to within a car length, and proved it one Sunday on the empty road behind the dairy, using a traffic cone she'd brought along in a carrier bag. Where does a woman who takes the bus get a traffic cone? I didn't ask.

Every lesson went the long way round, by the reservoir. The short way to anywhere was the bridge road, past the police station and the magistrates' court, and we never took it. I thought she had a thing about bridges. I asked her once and she said the reservoir road had better bends, which was true, it has eleven, and I can still drive them in my head when I can't sleep.

* * *

Pruitt lives below me. That September, before any of the bin bags, he got a parking ticket for thirty-five pounds outside the dentist's and took it the way other men take a diagnosis. I was coming in from the hospital, the evening visit, when he opened his door the width of the chain and said, "You're a measurements man."

I am, as it happens. My job with the county is checking that scales weigh what they say they weigh and that a pint is a pint. It comes with a set of brass weights in a fitted case and a laminated card that gets me into the back of any butcher's in the district. Pruitt wanted me to come and watch him measure the parking bay. He believed it was short, and that a bay under the regulation length wasn't legally a bay, and that a man couldn't overstay in a place that didn't exist.

So at seven the next morning I stood outside the dentist's holding the dumb end of a tape measure. The bay was eleven centimetres short. Pruitt photographed the tape against the kerb with that day's newspaper in the shot, like a kidnapper. He's devoted to Columbo, and he ran the whole appeal in that manner, which meant letters to the council that were humble for three paragraphs and then had just one more question. He asked me to sign a statement saying I'd witnessed the measurement. I said I couldn't sign it as an officer, because the tape wasn't a certified tape. "But it's right," Pruitt said. "It's probably right," I said. He looked at me as though I'd keyed his car.

I signed as a neighbour. Then I went to work, and then to the hospital, where visiting was two till four and six till eight, and where Ilse slept through most of both.

* * *

In 2003 she rang me at work, which she'd never done before, and said, "I'd like to go to Trieste, and I'd like to go by road, and you have a car." I was twenty-six. The car was a hatchback I'd bought third-hand, with one door from a different car in a different blue. I said most people would fly. She said most people were welcome to.

She never told me why Trieste. On the ferry she said only that it was a city that had been in the wrong country several times and had got on with things, and then she went to find the tea. That's a fair summary of the place, I've since learned, but it isn't a reason, and I don't have a better one to give you.

She navigated. She had a road atlas with the route inked in, and her own folded sheets for the towns, copied in pencil from library books, with the one-way streets arrowed. For four days she called every turn a full minute early. She was wrong once, in a town with a new bypass, and took it personally. The wrist system doesn't work from a map, so she talked, in the car, while it was moving, about the price of the tolls and the state of other people's lane discipline. I'd never heard her say so much in a row.

On the third afternoon, somewhere flat with a great many lorries, I'd been driving for six hours and my eyes were doing the thing where the white lines lift off the road. I pulled into a services and asked if she'd take the wheel for an hour. Just the motorway, just straight on. She looked at the steering wheel for a while, the way you'd look at a dog you'd been told was friendly. "No," she said. "You sleep for forty minutes and I'll keep watch, and after that I'll read to you." Which she did. She read Middlemarch aloud from the passenger seat for the better part of two hundred kilometres, doing the voices, all of them slightly cross.

I put it down to the foreign side of the road. My mother, when I told her afterwards, put it down to Ilse being Ilse, and moved on to whether we'd eaten properly.

In Trieste she walked to the end of the long stone pier that sticks out into the gulf, in a wind that took her headscarf clean off and gave it to the sea, and she watched it go without any sign of minding. We stayed two nights. She bought nothing. She navigated us home by a different route entirely, inked just as carefully in a second colour, which she'd evidently had ready all along.

* * *

I took my test in August 1994, on a Thursday, at ten past nine. Ilse came with me, in her good coat, in August, in the passenger seat all the way to the test centre by a route that took in the ring road and a retail park and left out the bridge. She waited on the bench outside with her handbag on her knees. The examiner was a Mr Coyle, who had a moustache and a clipboard and said about nine words in forty minutes, four of which were "emergency stop", twice.

I passed with two minor faults, both for hesitation. Both were at junctions, where I'd sat a beat too long with a gap in front of me and nobody's hand on my wrist.

When I came out, Ilse stood up. She skipped the well done and held out her hand for the paperwork. There were two bits: the pass certificate, which you posted off to get your licence, and the examiner's marking sheet, a flimsy carbon copy with my two faults crossed in biro. She gave the certificate straight back. The marking sheet she read on the front, and then turned over and read on the back, where there's nothing but small print about appeals. It took her a long time. Then she put it in her handbag and clicked the clasp and said, "I'll mind that for you." I had no use for it and never asked for it back.

My mother did a lunch. She'd put Brief Encounter on in the other room, as she did at Christmas and on any occasion she felt needed marking, and she cried at the station scenes on schedule. Ilse's view, given annually, was that it was a film about railway timetables and that everybody in it should have learned to drive. "You're one to talk," my mother said, and then she looked at the potatoes. I remember it because the potatoes didn't need looking at.

* * *

Pruitt won, by the way. The council cancelled the ticket in a two-line letter that admitted nothing about the bay, and he was miserable for a week. He'd wanted it in writing that he was right. Last I heard he was still writing to them about the eleven centimetres.

The second day at the flat I did the desk. Ilse's desk had four drawers and she'd labelled them in pencil on the inside lip: Bills, Letters, Guarantees, Other. Other had a brown envelope in it, the kind that closes with a string and a cardboard button, and in the envelope were five pieces of paper.

A provisional driving licence issued in 1971, the old green paper sort, long expired. Three test failure sheets, from 1971, 1972 and 1974, the last with nine crosses on it and a word in the examiner's handwriting that I think is "nerves" and might be "verges". And a flimsy carbon copy from August 1994 with two crosses for hesitation and my name at the top.

I went through the rest of the flat, lifting the drawer liners, looking for a full licence the way you look for a thing you already know isn't there. There wasn't one. For four months in 1994 I'd driven around on L-plates supervised, in the eyes of the law, by nobody, under the instruction of a woman who as far as any document shows hadn't sat behind a wheel in twenty years. And who knew the stopping distance of a Volvo 240 in the wet to within a car length. I still can't tell you how. I've thought about the traffic cone a good deal.

Here's the question you've got, because I had it too: did my mother know? She sent me to Ilse with a reference. She said the thing over the potatoes. Against that, my mother is a woman who once reported herself to the library over a book she had already returned. I could ring her. She's seventy-four, she picks up on the second ring, and she'd tell me. I'm not going to. You can think what you like about that, and about me, but you'll have to do it without the answer, the same as I do.

I put the five papers back in the order I'd found them, mine at the back, and did up the string.

* * *

The last time she was awake for me was a Tuesday in September, the evening visit, about an hour before Pruitt and his door chain.

They'd moved her to the bed by the window, which she'd have asked for if she'd been asking for things. Her book was on the locker with a hospital menu card in it for a bookmark. I sat on the side where her good hand was and told her about the scales at the new fishmonger's, which were out by six grams in the fishmonger's favour, because she liked a villain.

She didn't open her eyes. About twenty minutes in, her hand came across the blanket and found my wrist, the left one, and tapped it. Once, and then after a gap, once more. Or once, and then a tremor: she'd had a tremor in that hand since July. I've run it back more often than she ran back the tape. There's a version where it was two, and a version where it was one and one, and a version where it wasn't anything, a hand settling on the nearest warm object. I waited for her to do it again so I could count properly. She didn't.

At eight they rang the bell for the end of visiting. I went down to the car park and fed the machine three pounds forty and drove to the exit. The barrier went up. The road outside the hospital is a fast one, and the traffic comes round blind from the right. I put the indicator on and sat there with it ticking. A gap came, big enough for a bus. I checked my mirror.
