# The Salt Keeping

I came back to Vessick House in the last week of October, eleven years after I had sworn never to enter it again, and I came as everyone came to Orrin Holm, by the Strake, that mile of cobbled causeway which the sea gives up twice a day and twice a day takes back. Hobb the carter drove me over from Dunmere at the bottom of the ebb, with the wet sand shining to either side like the flank of some great skinned animal and the marker posts standing out of it, each wearing a ring of weed to show how high the water meant to come. He looked at the sky over the western bar, and said that the springs were on and the glass falling, and that if I meant to be off the Holm before Sunday I had better mean it soon.

The house had been going blind while I was away. Salt had done it: forty winters of spray flung up the cliff and dried upon the glass, so that every seaward window wore a cataract of grey-white crust through which the rooms behind could only be guessed at. It lay in the grooves of the step in a fine pale line, like the tidemark in a drained bath.

Mrs. Quill opened to me before I had let go the bell. She was a long, flat, colourless woman who seemed to have been pressed at some time between the pages of a heavy book. Her right hand was wound to the wrist in clean linen. "A scald, Miss Ismay," she said, seeing me look. "The kettle. You'll want to see him, but you'll want a fire first."

I followed her down the hall, and at the door of my father's study I stopped, because the hall there smelled of lamp oil—the sweetish, clinging, mineral reek of it. There had been no oil lamp in Vessick House in my lifetime. A lamp overturned in the nursery, the year before I was born, had taken the roof of the east wing, and from that day my father would have nothing under his slates but wax candles and the kitchen range. "You smell the sea, miss," said Mrs. Quill, without turning round. "It gets into everything." I was tired, and cold, and I let it be the sea.

* * *

They had laid him in the Blue Room. The blue of its name had long since left the walls and survived only in the folds of the bed-curtains, where the light had not reached, so that to draw one back was to uncover a stripe of deep and startling colour, like the blue in the hollow of a mussel shell. Four candles burned at the corners of the bed, and their flames leaned all one way, toward the chimney, drawn by a draught that came under the door and went up the cold flue with a sound like someone breathing through a comb; and with every lean the shadows of the bedposts swung together across the ceiling and swung apart, a slow black scissoring over the plaster vines and pomegranates that some forgotten Varrow had ordered from an Italian, and that the damp had been quietly unpicking ever since. The room smelled of beeswax, of cold linen, of the lavender water with which Mrs. Quill had washed him. Under the window a moth-eaten tiger, shot by a great-uncle, lay with its glass eyes full of candle.

My father lay in the middle of all this with his hands folded and a saucer of salt upon his breast. "It's only what's customary," Mrs. Quill said from the door. "Salt keeps. It keeps the flesh from swelling, and some will tell you it keeps the rest of him where it belongs until the ground has him. I don't say I hold with that. I put it there all the same." He had taken to his bed on the Friday week, she told me, and had gone in his sleep on the Sunday night; the doctor from Dunmere had signed his paper, and the parson would bury him on Saturday if the Strake allowed.

I had carried a quarrel to that house through eleven years, had kept it fed and in good repair, and had rehearsed in a great many rented rooms what I should say when at last he was obliged to listen: that a child of nine whose mother has drowned is owed more than silence; that he had let me grow up in a house where her name was never spoken and her portrait was taken down. And there he lay with his jaw bound up in a napkin, beyond the reach of every word of it, and what I felt, looking down at him, was cheated. It was the feeling of someone who has been robbed of a thing that was rightfully hers. I felt that I had been owed a hearing, and that by dying he had contrived to slip out of paying it. A sense of injustice came over me, the particular bitterness of being denied what I had waited so long to claim. I did not weep. I kissed his forehead, which was as cold as the step of his own front door, and went down to the fire.

* * *

The weather Hobb had read in the sky arrived that night, and those who have never lived at the mercy of a causeway must be told what that meant. The Strake lies bare, in ordinary seasons, for some three hours on either side of low water. But a spring tide ebbs shorter, and when a westerly gale stands behind it the whole weight of the bay is heaped into the channel and held there, so that the ebb never properly comes: the water draws down a little, sulkily, leaving the cobbles a foot under and the current across them running like a mill race, and then it turns and comes on again. On the Friday morning I went out to the head of the cliff path and looked down. There was no causeway. There was a brown, ridged, hurrying water from the Holm to the Dunmere shore, and the marker posts stood in it to their necks, each dragging a long crease of foam. No cart could cross that, nor any boat live in it; the Holm's one dinghy lay in any case stove in upon the slip, where a sea had dropped it in the night.

"Three days," said Mrs. Quill, when I came in streaming. "Four, if the wind stays where it is. We've flour and coals, and he'll keep, in that cold room." So I was shut up in Vessick with the dead man and his housekeeper, and I determined to go through his papers. The study door was locked. Mrs. Quill was very sorry: the master had kept that key upon his own chain, and the chain she had not been able to find. She met my eye as she said it for just as long as was natural.

* * *

By Saturday the gale had gone round into the north-east and thinned to a hard steady wind with sleet in it, which rattled on the crusted panes like thrown rice. The light that got through was the colour of dishwater and gave out altogether by three in the afternoon. It was the day we should have buried him, and I, who had not wept and did not expect to, watched the sky do it for me with a thoroughness I could never have matched.

I sat through most of it in the morning room, which had been my mother's. Her work-table was still there, with the silk bag hanging beneath it, rotted now to a few pink strings; the wallpaper had come away from the chimney-breast in one long tongue, showing the older paper behind, a pattern of wrens that I half remembered from the nursery. There was a pale oblong above the mantel where her portrait had hung. The very room seemed to grieve alongside me, its long neglect a likeness of the sorrow I carried within myself.

Toward noon, the sleet easing, I walked in the walled garden. The espaliers had torn loose from their wires and hung forward off the brick like men in the stocks; the box edgings had grown up into shapeless hedges and died back brown on the windward side, scorched by spray; and in the central bed the sundial had fallen from its pedestal and lay face down among the dead asters. Everywhere I looked the garden gave me back my own desolation, as though it too had lost someone and had let itself go to ruin out of grief.

I came in by the scullery, and there, on the rack beneath the stone sink, I found my father's walking boots. They were ruined: the leather gone hard as wood and pale to above the ankle, with a bloom of white crystals in every seam. I scraped one with my thumbnail and put the nail to my tongue. Those boots had stood in the sea, and deep, and no very long time ago—the linings were still damp—and they belonged to a man who by Mrs. Quill's account had kept his bed for nine days before he died in it. Salt, which I had thought of until that hour only as the thing that was eating the house, had turned witness.

* * *

I waited that night until I heard her go up. Then I took a candle and the morning-room poker, and levered at the study lock until the old wood gave. The smell came out to meet me like something that had been standing behind the door: lamp oil, rank and heavy.

The room had been stripped. My father's desk stood with every drawer pulled out and empty; the deed-boxes gaped along their shelf; and the wide grate was heaped to the bars with a sodden black mass of burnt paper, layered like leaf-mould, soaked with oil and badly burned even so. A two-gallon can lay on its side on the hearthrug. I knelt and turned the ashes over with the poker. It had all been letters. Here and there a corner survived with a line or two of a fine sloping hand I did not know, and once a foreign stamp.

"He did it himself," said Mrs. Quill behind me. "The Sunday. He had me order the oil a month back and never said what for. He was in here all that evening with the door locked, and I smelled it and beat upon the door, and when he opened it the chimney was roaring and he walked straight past me with his hat on. The tide was making. I stood at the top of the cliff path and watched him walk out along the Strake in the dusk until the water was at his knees, and then I couldn't see him any more. I found him at the next ebb against the fourth post, and brought him up on the garden barrow, and washed him, and put him to bed. They'd not have put him in the churchyard else."

I asked her whose the letters had been. "He said they were yours by rights, and that you were better without them. That's all he ever said." She was holding the bandaged hand against her chest, and I asked what she had truly done to it. "The kettle," said Mrs. Quill. "I told you."

On the Tuesday the Strake came up out of the water, and we buried my father in the lee of the church wall as a man who had died in his bed. I left by the same ebb. Mrs. Quill and I parted on the step with a civility that either of us might have broken with a word.

* * *

Vessick was sold. Mrs. Quill went to a niece in Dunmere, and for six years I believed I understood what had happened on the Holm: that my father, a cold man grown old, had made an end of his correspondence and then of himself, and that his housekeeper had lied for him out of loyalty.

Then, in the seventh spring, the niece wrote to tell me that her aunt was dead, and enclosed a flat packet sewn into oilcloth which had been found at the bottom of the salt crock in Mrs. Quill's kitchen, with my name on it in pencil. Inside was a single sheet of letter paper, brown and brittle along one edge where fire had been at it, and stiff throughout with salt, so that fine grains fell from the folds as I opened it. It was written in the sloping hand I had seen in the grate, and dated from Port Maurelle, in the March of the year I turned fifteen. It began "My dear Ambrose," and it asked—as the writer said she had asked in every letter for six years—only to be told whether Ismay was well, and whether she had been allowed to keep the wrens on the nursery wall. It was signed "Leonie."

My mother had not drowned on the Strake when I was nine. She had crossed it, on some ordinary ebb, and gone on living, and had written to that house year after year; and my father had kept every letter locked in his deed-boxes, and had told his daughter that the sea had taken her. Port Maurelle, when I enquired, remembered her: a foreign lady who gave lessons in drawing, and who died of a fever two winters before my father walked into the tide. I had gone to Vessick to reproach him for his silence about a drowned woman, and all through my childhood that silence had been hiding a living one.

What I cannot settle, and have stopped expecting to, is whose hand lit that fire. One page came out of the grate, and someone burned a hand to the wrist in fetching it. It may be that Mrs. Quill told me the truth, and snatched what she could when he had gone, and afterwards could neither give it to me nor destroy it. It may equally be that he went down to the sea with his boxes still locked, and that a woman who had kept his house for forty years, and his lie for twenty, carried the oil in herself to keep it a while longer, and repented of a single sheet. She packed it in salt either way, as she packed everything she meant to last. I keep the page as it came, in a jar of coarse grey salt on the sill of my window, a long way inland, and on wet days when the lid sweats I can taste the Holm on the air of the room.
